How to play pétanque

Updated 14 September 2026

Draft — references to the rulebook's articles have not been verified yet.

In one sentence. You throw a small jack, then each side tries to get its boules closest to it; at the end of an end, only one team scores, and the game is played to 13. The rest fits in seven minutes of reading — and one game.

What you need

Boules: metal, 70.5 to 80 mm across, 650 to 800 g in competition — leisure boules do perfectly well on a Sunday. A jack, also called the cochonnet: a small ball of wood or resin, about 30 mm. And a patch of more or less flat ground.

Each player has three boules in singles and in doubles, two in triples: a team always plays six boules. art. ?

In competition, play is on a marked terrain 15 m by 4 m. art. ?

A game is played to 13 points, in successive ends — in French, mènes: an end runs from the throw of the jack to the last boule played. The counter counts exactly that — a team, then a number of boules, at each end.

Holding the boule

This is what surprises everyone the first time: the hand is upside down compared with throwing a ball. The boule sits in the palm, fingers together, thumb along the side. At the moment of release, the palm faces the ground and the back of the hand faces the sky.

The arm starts from behind and swings from the shoulder, like a pendulum. The wrist unrolls last, and the fingers "close" on the boule as they let it go: that is what gives it backspin — it turns backwards in flight, and brakes when it touches the ground instead of running on.

One tip for the first game: do not aim at the jack, aim at the spot where the boule will land — the donnée. The ground will do the rest.

Feet in the circle

You throw from a circle: drawn on the ground, 35 to 50 cm inside diameter, or a rigid circle of 50 cm. It sits at least 1 m from any obstacle. art. ?

Both feet are entirely inside the circle, on the ground, not biting the line — and they stay there until the boule has touched the ground. art. ?

"Pieds tanqués", feet planted, in Provençal: that is where the word pétanque comes from. You may point crouching or standing, as you like, as long as the feet do not move.

Point or shoot

There are only two gestures. Pointing: placing your boule as close as possible to the jack. Shooting: knocking an opponent's boule away by hitting it.

In doubles there is often a pointer and a shooter; in triples, a pointer, a middle and a shooter. Nothing imposes it: it is a habit, and everyone plays the boules they want.

The choice is made boule by boule. If you can beat the best opposing boule, point. If it is too well placed, or blocks your way, shoot. A terrain saying, not a rule: you don't shoot a boule you can point past.

Three ways to point

The roll — roulette: the boule leaves low and rolls the whole way. For smooth, flat ground only — anywhere else, the smallest stone deflects it.

The half-lob — demi-portée: the boule lands about halfway and rolls the rest. The everyday throw.

The high lob — portée: the boule goes high and drops close to the jack, almost without rolling. For stony or sloping ground, or when an opposing boule blocks the road.

In all three cases you first choose the landing spot, then read the ground between the landing spot and the jack. Higher means less roll and less luck on bad ground — but more precision demanded at the landing.

The shot

You aim at the opposing boule — sometimes at the jack. You shoot standing, arm straight, the boule released harder and flatter than when pointing.

The carreau: your boule takes exactly the place of the one it knocks away. The perfect shot. The tir au fer: your boule strikes the target without touching the ground first. The reference shot. The rafle, or raspaille: the boule lands far in front and runs along the ground into the target. Allowed, but frowned upon — and missed as soon as the ground is not smooth.

After a shot, everything that moved stays where it stopped: that is the game. A boule that leaves the terrain entirely is dead, even if it comes back. art. ?

Before shooting, mark the position of the jack and the boules with a small line on the ground. Without a mark, no claim is possible. art. ?

An end, from the jack to the last boule

A coin toss decides who starts. That team draws the circle, throws the jack, then plays the first boule. art. ?

The jack is good if it stops between 6 and 10 m from the circle, at least 1 m from any obstacle and from the end line. art. ?

One throw only: if the jack is not good, the opponents place it by hand, anywhere within the limits — and the first boule stays with the team that threw the jack. art. ?

From then on, it is always the team that does not hold the point that plays: it plays until it takes the point back, or runs out of boules. When one team has no boules left, the other plays all of its own. art. ?

Everyone has one minute to play, counted from the moment the previous boule — or the jack — stopped, or the measurement was made. art. ?

The end is over when nobody has boules left. You count, and the team that scored throws the next jack from a circle drawn around where the jack was. art. ?

Counting and measuring

At the end of an end, only one team scores: one point per boule closer to the jack than the best opposing boule. art. ?

That is exactly what the counter asks, in that order: who scored, then how many boules. An end is worth 1 to 6 points, never anything else.

At the slightest doubt, measure — with a tape or a compass, never with your feet. The team that played the last boule measures. art. ?

While measuring, touch nothing: whoever moves the jack or a disputed boule loses the point. And nobody picks up a boule before the count is accepted by all. art. ?

If the two best boules are at equal distance from the jack and nobody has boules left, the end is void: nothing is scored, and the team that threw the jack starts again. art. ?

The game stops at 13. Boules placed beyond that are written on the sheet but count for nothing — the counter already knows: the button that leads to 13 says "game", and nothing goes further.

When the jack is dead

The jack is dead when it leaves the terrain entirely — even if it comes back —, when it can no longer be seen from the circle, or when it is pushed more than 20 m from the circle or less than 3 m. art. ?

What follows depends on the boules left. If both teams still have boules, or neither does, the end is void: nobody scores. If only one team still has boules, it scores as many points as it has left to play. art. ?

It is the most common scoresheet mistake: "jack out, void end" is true only half the time. The counter does not guess this case — it is up to you to enter the points, or to skip the end.

On the terrain

Between the circle and the jack, the end is alive: nobody walks there, nobody crosses in front of the player throwing. Opponents stand to the side or behind, more than 2 m away, and in silence. art. ?

A boule weighs 700 g and a shot travels fast. Children and dogs off the terrain, the boules you have not yet played away from the circle, and look before you cross.

The phone: in official competition it is forbidden during the game — that is article 39 of the rules, the red card in the terms. The counter is made for Sunday games, practice and friendly tournaments.

The rules, and the counter

This page tells the rules in plain words; it replaces neither the rulebook nor an umpire. The text that counts is the Official rules for the sport of pétanque of the international federation (FIPJP) — the French text is the original, and an official English edition exists on fipjp.org — and in competition the umpire decides. The "art." references above point to the edition we checked, on the date at the top of the page.

And the counter does the one thing left: keeping the score, two taps per end.
